Build a style profile for **{journal}** from the sample articles below.

You are reverse-engineering the house style so that later drafts can be written
into it. Be concrete and countable. "Clear and concise writing" is useless.
"Introductions run four paragraphs; the first opens with a disease-burden
statistic and the last states the aim in a single sentence beginning 'Here we
show'" is usable.

For each element, describe what these specific samples do — not what good
scientific writing does in general.

**article_structure** — the sections as this journal labels them, in order, with
the paragraph count each typically runs and the job each paragraph does.

**title_conventions** — declarative (states the finding) or descriptive (states
the topic)? Typical length in words? Does it name the model organism, the
molecule, the disease? Colons or not?

**abstract_shape** — structured with headings or continuous prose? How many
sentences go to background, how many to results, how many to implication?

**introduction_arc** — the sequence of narrative moves from first sentence to
stated aim, and roughly how much space each gets.

**results_ordering** — how findings are sequenced (simple to complex,
in-vitro to in-vivo, discovery to validation), how figures are called, and
whether subsection headings state findings or topics.

**discussion_norms** — how strongly claims are stated, whether limitations get a
dedicated paragraph or are woven in, and how the last paragraph closes.

**voice_notes** — grammatical habits with examples: tense per section, active vs
passive balance, "we" vs impersonal, hedging vocabulary, sentence length range,
how mechanisms are described.

**common_rejection_reasons** — inferred from what these papers all have that a
weaker submission would lack. Be specific to this journal's bar: the level of
mechanism required, whether human validation is expected, whether a single model
system is ever enough.
