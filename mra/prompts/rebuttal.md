Draft the point-by-point response to these reviews.

The failure mode of this task is agreeing with everything. A letter that
concedes every point either promises experiments nobody will run, or gives away
a claim the data actually supported. Reviewers notice both. Your job is to sort
the comments first and write second.

## Sort every comment into one of five

**concede-and-do** — they are right and it needs new work. Say exactly what
work, in `new_work`, at a level of detail the authors could cost out. Never
write "we will perform additional experiments".

**concede-and-limit** — they are right, and the honest answer is a stated
limitation, not more experiments. Use this when the experiment they want is
impossible with the cohort, would take longer than the revision window, or
would not actually settle the question. Say why in the response; a reviewer
accepts a reasoned no far more often than a vague yes.

**already-addressed** — the paper answers this and the reviewer did not find
it. **This is a writing failure, not a reading failure.** The response says
where it is *and* what you are changing so the next reader cannot miss it.
A bare "please see page 7" invites the same comment again from the editor.

**disagree** — the objection does not hold: they misread a number, applied a
standard from a different design, or asked for a control that tests something
else. Say so plainly and give the ground. Politeness is in the register, not in
pretending to agree. If you cannot name the specific ground, it is not a
disagree — reclassify it.

**out-of-scope** — a fair question about the next paper. Draw the boundary and
say what this paper does claim. Do not promise it as "future work" unless the
authors would actually do it.

## Rules

**Quote the comment verbatim.** `quote` is checked mechanically against the
letter. Copy the reviewer's words; do not clean up their grammar, do not
paraphrase. Truncate a long comment rather than compressing it.

**`asks_for` is the real request, not the literal one.** "The statistics are
inappropriate" usually means they do not believe the effect survives the
correct test. "Please discuss X" often means they think X explains your result.
Answer the real one.

**Severity is about the main claim, not about tone.** A politely worded comment
that dissolves the central conclusion is `fatal`. A rude comment about figure
font size is `minor`. Reviewers' confidence and reviewers' correctness are
unrelated.

**Cite only what is in the supplied literature.** Every identifier in
`evidence` must appear below, in that exact form. A fabricated citation in a
response letter is worse than no citation: the editor can check it in seconds,
and it costs the authors the benefit of the doubt on every other point.

**Never write a number that is not in the manuscript or the data.** If a
response needs a statistic the authors have not supplied, say so in `new_work`
instead of inventing it.

**`risk`** — assume this reviewer reads your response and is not satisfied.
What do they say next? Write that. An answer that only works if nobody pushes
is not an answer.

**`threats`** — list the comments that endanger the main conclusion no matter
how the response is worded. If one of these exists and you do not say so, the
authors find out from the editor instead. Leave it empty only when it is
genuinely empty.

**`decisions`** — the authors have to choose: run the experiment or reframe the
claim, drop the figure or defend it, revise or appeal. Phrase each as the
question they must answer, not as your recommendation.
