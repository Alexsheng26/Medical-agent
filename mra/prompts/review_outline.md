Plan a review article on **{topic}**, using only the papers below.

You are planning, not writing. The output is a structure the researcher will
read and correct before anything is drafted.

## What makes this outline good

**Every section must make an argument, not cover a topic.** "TREM2 in liver
disease" is a topic and tells the writer nothing. "TREM2 expression marks the
recruited rather than the resident macrophage compartment, which changes what
its correlation with fibrosis means" is an argument, and a section can be
written from it.

**Assign papers to sections.** Each section lists the ids it is answerable
from, taken from the retrieved context and nowhere else. A section with no ids
cannot be written and must not be proposed. If a paper is central it may appear
in more than one section.

**Name the disagreements.** This is the part most reviews get wrong and the part
a reader relies on. Where two stored papers point in opposite directions, say so
in `controversies` with both ids, and make sure some section is responsible for
addressing it. A review that reports only the majority direction is worse than
no review: it launders a live dispute into apparent consensus.

**Say what the corpus cannot support.** `unsupported` is for claims a reader
would expect this review to make — because the field makes them — that these
particular papers do not establish. Naming them here stops them being written
later, and tells the researcher what to go and read next.

**Scope honestly.** If the knowledge base holds twelve papers, this is a focused
review of a narrow question, not a comprehensive survey. Say which it is in
`scope`. Do not pad the outline to look complete.

## Structure

4 to 8 sections. A workable spine, when the material allows it:

1. the clinical or biological problem, and why it is unresolved
2. what is established, with the strength of the evidence stated
3. the mechanism as currently proposed, step by step
4. where the evidence conflicts, and what would settle it
5. what this means for practice or for the next experiment
6. what remains open

Depart from this whenever the papers suggest a better order. An outline that
follows the evidence beats one that follows a template.
